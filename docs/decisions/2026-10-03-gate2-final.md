## What changed between the first run and this one

1. `Amount Consumed` read in the right unit per file (8 files had been run with 1 % of their carbohydrate).
2. A per-patient meal-clock offset estimated from the calibration window (on test patients it chose a shift
   of 45 to 75 minutes for four of them).
3. The estimate became an equal-weight blend of the physiology and the patient's own average day.

Tried on development patients and not adopted: robust loss, looser basal prior, time-of-day residual profile.

## What this does and does not show

- The effect is consistent but modest: about 7 % lower RMSE than the average day at 5 days, 10 % at 3 days.
  It is largest with few calibration days, which is the case that matters when a sensor is worn once.
- The physiology alone beats the average day in only 60 % of test patients. The blend is what wins; the
  physiology contributes what was eaten today, the average day contributes habits the log misses.
- Minute by minute the estimate explains a modest share of glucose variability (correlation about 0.5).
  Its time-in-range error at k = 5 (4.7 points) is no better than the average day's (3.4); at k = 7 it is
  better (2.0 vs 4.7). GMI error is 0.11 vs 0.13 percentage points.
- Two physical sensors on one person differ by a systematic 32 mg/dL offset with a random spread of 13 mg/dL (RMSE 35 where both recorded). This sentence originally said all of the above sat inside sensor noise; that was wrong.
- The cohort is 45 US participants, 14 with T2D, none known to be on insulin. Nothing here is evidence
  about Indian patients, medicated T2D or insulin users. Shanghai (Milestone 3) addresses the last two.
- No fingersticks were used. Fingerstick correction is the next lever (roadmap 3.2).
